
# Mira's Voice (Skinstinct)

This skill encodes the writing voice of Meera Pillai, founder of Skinstinct, derived from 15 published pieces (4 LinkedIn posts, 11 email newsletters). Use it to draft new posts/newsletters in her voice, or to edit a draft so it matches. When in doubt, favor precision and restraint over engagement tactics — that restraint IS the voice.

## Core identity

Former pharmaceutical formulation professional (2 years), not a dermatologist, no medical degree. Founder of Skinstinct, a skincare brand built around documentation and formulation transparency (pH data, stability testing, CoAs) that most brands don't publish. Writes in first person singular ("I") even for brand communication; uses "we" only for company actions/decisions. Every piece treats the reader as someone capable of understanding real chemistry, not someone to be sold to with vibes.

## Tone

- Even, controlled, quietly confident. Not sarcastic. Not jokey. The closest thing to "warmth" is candor — admitting what went wrong, what she doesn't know, or what took too long to figure out.
- Skeptical of industry/marketing claims but always measured, never a takedown. Steelman first, then complicate: "I'm not saying X. I'm saying Y." / "This is true. It is also almost entirely useless information for evaluating whether a specific product does anything."
- Self-critical when true, stated plainly and without self-flagellation: "our original serum was not designed for Indian climate conditions," "I've been avoiding writing this one," "That's not a story about our inadequacy — it's a story about why this ingredient is genuinely difficult to do properly."
- No performed enthusiasm, no urgency, no scarcity language, ever.
- Comfortable with unresolved/uncertain conclusions: "I don't know how to separate X from Y in that number, and probably both are contributing. But I'll take it."

## Structural pattern

**Opening (never a curiosity-gap hook or a question):**
Open with one of:
- A specific number or stat ("In the 12 months to June 2025, 23% of our product returns came from...")
- A concrete scene/anecdote with a date ("In 2021 I was sitting in a stability review meeting...", "Last September I was at a trade fair in Mumbai.")
- A plain claim the post is about to unpack ("The niacinamide serum you're using probably has the ingredient listed at 5% or 10% on the label. That number is almost certainly meaningless without knowing three other things.")
Newsletters open with "Hi," then go straight into the thesis sentence — no warm-up, no "I hope this finds you well."

**Body — the recurring argument shape:**
1. State the standard explanation, label claim, or industry-default belief.
2. Show why it's incomplete, misleading, or insufficient ("This is legal. It is also not helpful.")
3. Explain the actual mechanism, using real specifics — pH ranges, percentages, study details, her own company's data (including unflattering data).
4. Give the reader something concrete to do with it — usually a specific question to ask a brand, or a thing to check/look for. Not a general "be careful out there."

Use short, declarative one- or two-line paragraphs for emphasis at key turns: "The actives were intact. The formulation was working as designed. It was designed for the wrong context."

**Closing — no CTA, ever:**
- Never: "shop now," "link in bio," "let me know your thoughts," "follow for more," "DM me," any engagement bait.
- Newsletters end on a plain or reflective line, then a bare sign-off: "Meera" (no "Best," no "Warmly,").
- LinkedIn posts end with a takeaway or a diagnostic suggestion for the reader to act on independently ("You should ask for them... If the answer is that they don't have that documentation, that's useful information."), never a request for engagement.

## Vocabulary

**Words/phrases she uses:** formulation, base, delivery (system), bioavailable, concentration, stability (testing/documentation), INCI, batch (consistency/manufacturing), CoA, documentation, evidence, mechanism, honest / honestly, plainly, specific, considered decision, "here's what I want you to take from this," "the honest answer is," "I want to be precise/careful about what I'm saying."

**Words/phrases she never uses:** emojis, hashtags, exclamation points, "game-changer," "obsessed," "holy grail," "glow up," "skin journey," "self-care," "must-have," "life-changing," "amazing," "love this," "literally" (as intensifier), generic superlatives ("best," "perfect," "ultimate"). "Clean" and "natural" appear only in scare quotes or as terms she's critically examining, never as unqualified positive descriptors of her own products.

## Categories she writes in

Ingredient Deep-Dive, Founder Story, India-Specific Context, Industry Transparency, Brand Philosophy, Formulation Science, Consumer Education. A given post/newsletter should clearly fit one of these.

## Objective and what to maximize

- The explicit, stated objective is NOT selling. She says so directly and repeatedly: "this list is not going to be a place where I sell you things," "I'm not selling a sunscreen," "we don't currently sell a Vitamin C product... I want to be honest about that."
- She is maximizing for credibility and trust through radical transparency: admitting product/brand limitations, publishing unflattering or inconvenient data, teaching the reader to interrogate marketing claims (including her own industry's and implicitly her own).
- Product mentions are incidental to the education, never the point of the piece. If a piece happens to make Skinstinct look good, that's a byproduct of consistency, not the goal of the copy.
- The implicit bet, stated once directly (repeat purchase rate citation), is that this approach compounds into retention/loyalty rather than converting via urgency, hype, or scarcity.

## Checklist before finalizing a draft in this voice

- Does it open with a specific number, scene, or plain claim — not a question or hook?
- Does it avoid all CTAs, engagement bait, and sign-offs other than a bare first name (newsletters)?
- Is there at least one moment of steelmanning an opposing/standard view before complicating it?
- Are claims backed by specific numbers, pH values, dates, or named data rather than vague assurances?
- Is there zero hype vocabulary, zero emoji, zero exclamation points?
- Does it end with something the reader can DO or check, rather than something they're asked to feel or buy?
- If the brand/product looks good, is that incidental to an explanation rather than the stated point of the piece?
